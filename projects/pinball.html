Pinball Machine Prototype

Overview

I designed and built a fully playable electromechanical pinball machine featuring real-time scoring, optical and impact sensing, solenoid-actuated flippers, motorized obstacles, multi-LED lighting effects, and a custom display system. The system integrated mechanical components, embedded electronics, and firmware into a complete interactive game.

My Role

I owned key aspects of the mechanical and software design, including:

  • Firmware (C++ / Arduino):
    Wrote event-driven firmware for real-time sensor handling, score updates, servo/DC motor control, light patterns, and gameplay logic.
  • CAD and mechanical design:
    Contributed to the full playfield layout, obstacle geometry, solenoid and motor mounts, brackets, and enclosures using OnShape; fabricated components via 3D printing and laser cutting.
  • Debugging, wiring & integration:
    Assembled and organized wiring across multiple subsystems, validated sensors, tested power distribution, and debugged issues with noise, weak sensor signals, and intermittent connections.

System Architecture & Key Features

Sensing & Game Input

  • Optical Sensors (IR break beams):
    Used to detect ball entry/exit in scoring regions. Each sensor fed into a non-inverting amplifier followed by a hysteretic comparator, producing stable digital signals for the Arduino.
  • Impact Sensors (Piezo elements):
    Placed under bumpers to detect ball impacts. Signals were conditioned with a high-pass filter and amplifier to isolate impact events from ambient vibrations.

Actuators & Motion

  • Solenoid Flippers:
    Flippers were driven using a transistorized solenoid circuit. Physical buttons fed directly into the gate, allowing immediate mechanical response without firmware delay.
  • Servos for moving obstacles:
    Two RC servos controlled mechanical obstacles and were driven directly from the Arduino.
  • Motorized elements:
    A DC motor was driven through a MOSFET gate-driver stage, receiving a PWM control signal from the microcontroller for variable speed effects.

Display & Lighting

  • Score Display:
    7-segment displays were controlled through shift registers for efficient pin usage and synchronized score updates.
  • LED Life Indicator System:
    Used LEDs to represent the player’s remaining lives. Each LED was driven through a BJT transistor stage, allowing the Arduino to control them reliably. The system illuminated the appropriate number of LEDs based on the current game state, providing a simple and clear life indicator.

Power System

  • AC-to-DC wall power fed into a switch, then routed through a set of LDO voltage regulators for stable supply rails. Decoupling capacitors were placed throughout to reduce noise and stabilize the analog sections.

Challenges & What I Learned

Sensor Noise & Signal Reliability

Piezo sensors produced extremely weak and inconsistent signals. We solved this by:

  • Adding dedicated op-amp amplification stages
  • Using comparators for clean edges
  • Adjusting firmware thresholds and timing filters
  • Iteratively stress-testing with actual gameplay

This taught me embedded signal conditioning, hardware/firmware co-design, and systematic debugging for mixed-signal systems.

Mechanical Integration & CAD Tolerances

Designing 3D-printed flipper mounts, servo brackets, and sensor housings taught me how tolerances, material flex, and fastener choices affect real-world fit, alignment, and durability.

Event-Driven Firmware Architecture

The game loop required fast, asynchronous handling of multiple sensors and actuators. I learned to structure firmware around events, non-blocking input handling, state machines, and consistent timing.

Hardware Debugging Under Constraints

Using breadboards led to intermittent connections that we had to trace and fix. Though simple, this taught me discipline in systematic wiring, power tracing, continuity testing, and subsystem isolation—skills crucial in embedded prototyping.
